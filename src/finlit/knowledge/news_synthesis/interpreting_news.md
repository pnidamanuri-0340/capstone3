# Interpreting Financial News

## Earnings reports
Public companies report earnings quarterly. Key items are revenue, earnings per share (EPS), and guidance (management's forecast). Stock reactions depend on results versus analyst expectations, not on absolute numbers: a company can report record profits and still fall if results or guidance miss expectations.

## The Federal Reserve and interest rates
The Federal Reserve sets the federal funds target rate to pursue maximum employment and stable prices (about 2% inflation). Rate hikes tend to raise borrowing costs, pressure bond prices, and can weigh on growth-stock valuations. Rate cuts generally do the opposite. Markets often move on changes in expected future policy rather than the decision itself.

## Inflation reports
The Consumer Price Index (CPI) and Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) index measure inflation. Core measures exclude volatile food and energy prices. Higher-than-expected inflation can raise expectations of tighter Fed policy.

## Jobs reports
The monthly employment report includes nonfarm payrolls and the unemployment rate. Strong job growth signals a healthy economy but can also raise inflation and rate concerns, so market reactions can seem counterintuitive.

## GDP and recessions
Gross domestic product measures total economic output. A common informal definition of recession is two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth, though in the U.S. recessions are officially dated by the NBER based on broad measures.

## News sentiment scores
Some data providers assign sentiment scores to articles, from bearish to bullish. These are automated estimates of tone and should not be treated as predictions or trading signals.

## Headlines vs substance
Headlines are written to attract attention. Words like "plunge" or "soar" may describe moves of 1% to 2%. Check the actual numbers, the time frame, and whether the move is meaningful relative to normal volatility.

## Source quality
Prefer primary sources (company filings at sec.gov, Federal Reserve statements, Bureau of Labor Statistics releases) and established financial outlets. Be skeptical of anonymous social media tips, guaranteed-return claims, and pump-and-dump promotions.

## Putting news in context for long-term investors
Most daily news has little impact on a diversified long-term investor's plan. Reacting to headlines by frequently trading tends to increase costs and taxes and can hurt returns. News is most useful for understanding why markets move and whether anything fundamental has changed.

## Analyst ratings and price targets
Analyst "buy/hold/sell" ratings and price targets reflect individual analysts' estimates and frequently change. They can be biased and are not guarantees.
