# Portfolio Allocation and Performance Metrics

## Strategic asset allocation
Strategic allocation sets long-term target weights (for example 60% stocks / 40% bonds) based on goals, horizon and risk tolerance, then periodically rebalances back to them. A traditional rule of thumb is "110 minus age" in stocks, though personal circumstances matter more than formulas. Target-date funds automate a glide path that shifts from stocks to bonds as the target year approaches.

## Common model portfolios
A three-fund portfolio holds a total U.S. stock market fund, a total international stock fund and a total U.S. bond fund. A 60/40 portfolio holds 60% stocks and 40% bonds and is a common moderate-risk benchmark. Aggressive portfolios may hold 80% to 100% stocks; conservative ones 20% to 40%.

## Rebalancing
Rebalancing restores target weights after market moves cause drift. Calendar rebalancing happens on a schedule (such as annually); threshold rebalancing happens when an asset drifts beyond a band (such as 5 percentage points). Rebalancing in tax-advantaged accounts or with new contributions avoids realizing taxable gains.

## Total return and annualized return
Total return measures the overall percentage change in value including dividends over a period. Annualized return (CAGR) converts a multi-period return into a yearly rate: (ending / starting)^(1 / years) - 1. Annualizing short periods (a few weeks) can exaggerate results and should be interpreted with caution.

## Volatility (standard deviation)
Annualized volatility is the standard deviation of periodic returns scaled by the square root of periods per year (about 252 trading days). U.S. large-cap stocks have historically shown roughly 15% to 20% annualized volatility; investment-grade bonds roughly 4% to 7%.

## Sharpe ratio
The Sharpe ratio equals (portfolio return minus risk-free rate) divided by volatility. It measures return per unit of risk. Above 1 is generally considered good for long periods; it should be compared between portfolios over the same period, not in isolation.

## Maximum drawdown
Maximum drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough decline over a period. It describes the worst loss an investor would have experienced, which is often more intuitive than volatility. The S&P 500 fell about 34% in early 2020 and about 57% from 2007 to 2009.

## Concentration risk
Holding a large share of a portfolio in one stock or sector increases risk. Many planners suggest keeping any single stock below about 5% to 10% of a portfolio. Employer stock in a 401(k) is a common source of concentration.

## Correlation
Correlation measures how two assets move together, from -1 to +1. Combining assets with low or negative correlation (such as stocks and high-quality bonds historically) can reduce portfolio volatility without proportionally reducing return.

## Benchmarks
A benchmark is an index used to evaluate performance, such as the S&P 500 for U.S. large caps or a blended 60/40 index for a balanced portfolio. Comparing to an appropriate benchmark shows whether active choices added value.

## Limitations of historical metrics
All performance metrics are backward-looking. Past returns, volatility and drawdowns do not guarantee future results, and short measurement windows can be misleading.
