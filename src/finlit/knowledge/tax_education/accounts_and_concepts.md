# U.S. Tax Concepts and Tax-Advantaged Accounts

Figures below are for U.S. federal tax year 2025. Limits are adjusted annually; verify current figures at irs.gov. This is general education, not tax advice.

## Marginal vs effective tax rate
The U.S. uses progressive brackets: each rate applies only to income within its bracket. The marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar of income. The effective rate is total tax divided by total income and is always lower than the top marginal rate. Moving into a higher bracket does not raise the tax on income in lower brackets.

## 2025 federal income tax brackets (single filers)
10% up to $11,925; 12% from $11,926 to $48,475; 22% to $103,350; 24% to $197,300; 32% to $250,525; 35% to $626,350; 37% above. Married filing jointly thresholds are roughly double up through the 32% bracket.

## Standard deduction vs itemizing
Taxpayers subtract either the standard deduction or itemized deductions (such as mortgage interest, state and local taxes up to a cap, and charitable gifts), whichever is larger. For 2025 the standard deduction is $15,750 for single filers and $31,500 for married filing jointly.

## Deductions vs credits
A deduction reduces taxable income; its value depends on your marginal rate (a $1,000 deduction saves $220 in the 22% bracket). A credit reduces tax owed dollar for dollar. Refundable credits can produce a refund even if no tax is owed.

## Traditional 401(k) and 403(b)
Workplace plans where contributions are typically pre-tax, reducing current taxable income; growth is tax-deferred and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. The 2025 employee contribution limit is $23,500, plus a $7,500 catch-up for age 50+ (a higher $11,250 catch-up applies at ages 60 to 63). Employer matches do not count toward the employee limit. Withdrawals before 59 1/2 generally incur a 10% penalty plus income tax, with exceptions.

## Roth 401(k)
Same contribution limits as a traditional 401(k), but contributions are after-tax and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

## Traditional IRA
Individual retirement account. The 2025 contribution limit is $7,000 ($8,000 if 50 or older), shared across traditional and Roth IRAs. Contributions may be tax-deductible depending on income and whether you or a spouse have a workplace plan. Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.

## Roth IRA
Contributions are after-tax; qualified withdrawals of earnings after age 59 1/2 and once the account has been open five years are tax-free. Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn anytime without tax or penalty. Eligibility phases out at higher incomes: for 2025, $150,000 to $165,000 MAGI for single filers and $236,000 to $246,000 for married filing jointly. Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions for the original owner.

## Traditional vs Roth: how to think about it
Traditional accounts give a tax break now; Roth accounts give tax-free income later. Roth tends to be favorable if you expect a higher tax rate in retirement than today (common for early-career, lower-income workers); traditional tends to be favorable if you expect a lower rate later. Many people use both for tax diversification.

## Health Savings Account (HSA)
Available with a high-deductible health plan. HSAs are triple tax-advantaged: deductible contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses. 2025 limits are $4,300 for self-only and $8,550 for family coverage, plus $1,000 catch-up at 55+. After 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed as income without penalty.

## 529 education plans
Earnings grow tax-free and withdrawals for qualified education expenses are tax-free federally. Up to $10,000 per year can be used for K-12 tuition. Unused funds may, under conditions, be rolled into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary (lifetime limit $35,000).

## Capital gains
Selling an investment for more than its cost basis produces a capital gain. Short-term gains (assets held one year or less) are taxed as ordinary income. Long-term gains (held more than one year) get preferential rates of 0%, 15% or 20%. For 2025 the 0% rate applies to taxable income up to $48,350 (single) or $96,700 (married filing jointly). A 3.8% net investment income tax can apply at higher incomes.

## Dividends
Qualified dividends are taxed at long-term capital gains rates; ordinary (non-qualified) dividends are taxed as ordinary income.

## Tax-loss harvesting and the wash-sale rule
Tax-loss harvesting sells investments at a loss to offset gains; up to $3,000 of net losses per year can offset ordinary income, with the rest carried forward. The wash-sale rule disallows the loss if you buy a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale.

## Required minimum distributions (RMDs)
Owners of traditional IRAs and 401(k)s must begin taking RMDs at age 73 (rising to 75 for those born in 1960 or later). Missing an RMD can trigger an excise tax.

## Asset location
Asset location places tax-inefficient investments (such as taxable bonds or REITs) in tax-advantaged accounts and tax-efficient ones (such as broad index ETFs) in taxable accounts to reduce overall taxes.
