# Core Personal Finance Concepts

## Budgeting and the 50/30/20 guideline
A budget is a plan for spending and saving income. A common starting guideline is 50/30/20: about 50% of after-tax income for needs (housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum debt payments), 30% for wants (dining out, entertainment, travel), and 20% for savings and extra debt repayment. It is a rule of thumb, not a law; people in high-cost areas or with high debt often adjust the ratios. Zero-based budgeting assigns every dollar a job so income minus planned spending and saving equals zero.

## Emergency fund
An emergency fund is cash set aside for unexpected expenses such as job loss, medical bills, or car repairs. A common target is three to six months of essential expenses, kept in a safe, liquid place such as a high-yield savings account or money market fund. People with variable income or a single household income often aim for the higher end.

## Compound interest
Compound interest means earning returns on both the original money (principal) and on previously earned returns. The formula is A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where P is principal, r the annual rate, n compounding periods per year, and t years. Time is the most powerful variable: money invested earlier has more periods to compound. The Rule of 72 estimates doubling time: divide 72 by the annual percentage return (at 8%, money doubles in about 9 years).

## APR vs APY
APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly interest rate without compounding and is typically quoted on loans and credit cards. APY (annual percentage yield) includes the effect of compounding and is quoted on savings accounts. APY = (1 + APR/n)^n - 1. A 5% APR compounded monthly is about a 5.12% APY.

## Inflation and real returns
Inflation is the general rise in prices over time, which reduces purchasing power. The real return approximately equals the nominal return minus inflation. A savings account paying 4% while inflation runs 3% has a real return of about 1%. Cash held long term tends to lose purchasing power.

## Credit scores
A FICO credit score ranges from 300 to 850. Major factors are payment history (about 35%), amounts owed / credit utilization (about 30%), length of credit history (about 15%), new credit (about 10%), and credit mix (about 10%). Keeping utilization below roughly 30% (lower is better) and paying on time are the most important habits. Checking your own score is a soft inquiry and does not hurt it.

## Good debt vs bad debt and payoff methods
Debt with a low interest rate that funds an appreciating asset or higher income (such as a reasonable mortgage or education) is sometimes called "good debt"; high-interest consumer debt such as credit card balances is costly. The avalanche method pays extra toward the highest-interest debt first and minimizes total interest. The snowball method pays the smallest balance first for motivational wins. Both require paying minimums on all debts.

## Net worth
Net worth equals total assets (cash, investments, home equity, etc.) minus total liabilities (loans, credit card balances). Tracking it over time is a simple measure of financial progress.
