# Investing Terms Glossary

## Stocks (equities)
A stock is a share of ownership in a company. Shareholders can benefit from price appreciation and dividends but bear the risk that the price falls. Stocks historically have had higher long-run returns than bonds or cash, with higher volatility.

## Bonds (fixed income)
A bond is a loan to a government or company that pays periodic interest (the coupon) and returns principal at maturity. Bond prices move inversely to interest rates: when rates rise, existing bond prices fall. Duration measures that sensitivity; a bond fund with duration 6 would lose roughly 6% if rates rose 1 percentage point. Credit risk is the risk the issuer fails to pay.

## Mutual funds, index funds, and ETFs
A mutual fund pools investor money into a portfolio managed to an objective; it is priced once per day at net asset value (NAV). An index fund tracks a market index such as the S&P 500 instead of picking securities, usually at low cost. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a fund that trades on an exchange throughout the day like a stock; many ETFs are index funds. ETFs are often more tax-efficient than mutual funds because of their creation/redemption mechanism.

## Expense ratio
The expense ratio is the annual fee a fund charges, expressed as a percentage of assets. A 0.03% expense ratio costs $3 per year per $10,000 invested; a 1% ratio costs $100. Over decades, fee differences compound into large differences in ending wealth.

## Diversification
Diversification means spreading money across many investments so that the poor performance of one has limited impact. It reduces unsystematic (company-specific) risk but cannot remove systematic (market-wide) risk.

## Asset allocation
Asset allocation is the mix of asset classes (stocks, bonds, cash, real estate) in a portfolio. It is the main driver of a portfolio's risk and return profile. Younger investors with long horizons often hold more stocks; those nearer to needing the money typically hold more bonds and cash.

## Dividends and dividend yield
A dividend is a cash payment from a company's profits to shareholders. Dividend yield equals annual dividends per share divided by share price. A very high yield can signal that the market expects a dividend cut.

## Price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio
The P/E ratio equals share price divided by earnings per share. It shows how much investors pay per dollar of earnings. A high P/E may reflect expected growth or overvaluation; it should be compared within an industry and against history.

## Market capitalization
Market cap equals share price times shares outstanding. Large-cap is typically above about $10 billion, mid-cap $2 to $10 billion, and small-cap below about $2 billion.

## Volatility and beta
Volatility is how much an investment's returns vary, usually measured by standard deviation. Beta measures sensitivity to the overall market: a beta of 1.2 means the stock historically moved about 20% more than the market.

## Dollar-cost averaging
Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price, which buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when high. It reduces the risk of investing a lump sum at a bad moment and builds a habit, though lump-sum investing has historically come out ahead more often when markets rise.

## Bull and bear markets
A bull market is a sustained rise in prices; a bear market is commonly defined as a decline of 20% or more from a recent peak. A correction is a decline of 10% to 20%.

## Risk tolerance and time horizon
Risk tolerance is the ability and willingness to endure declines in portfolio value. Time horizon is how long until the money is needed. Money needed within about three to five years is generally not suited to heavy stock exposure.
