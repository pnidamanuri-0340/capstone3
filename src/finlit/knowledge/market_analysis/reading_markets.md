# Reading Stock Quotes and Market Data

## Anatomy of a quote
A stock quote shows the latest price, the change and percent change from the previous close, the day's open, high and low, and volume (number of shares traded). The previous close is the final price of the prior trading session. Quotes from free data sources may be delayed and are for informational purposes.

## Volume
Volume is the number of shares traded in a period. Price moves on unusually high volume are often viewed as more significant than moves on light volume. Average daily volume also indicates liquidity: how easily shares can be bought or sold without moving the price.

## Bid, ask and spread
The bid is the highest price a buyer will pay; the ask is the lowest price a seller will accept. The difference is the spread. Highly traded large-cap stocks typically have narrow spreads; thinly traded stocks can have wide ones.

## Market, limit and stop orders
A market order executes immediately at the best available price. A limit order executes only at a specified price or better. A stop order becomes a market order once a trigger price is reached and is often used to limit losses.

## 52-week range
The 52-week high and low show the highest and lowest prices over the past year. Where the current price sits within that range gives context but is not, by itself, a buy or sell signal.

## Valuation metrics
Common valuation ratios include P/E (price to earnings), forward P/E (based on estimated earnings), P/B (price to book value), P/S (price to sales) and PEG (P/E divided by earnings growth rate). They are most useful when compared with peers in the same industry and with a company's own history.

## Comparing multiple stocks
When comparing stocks, look at the same metrics side by side: percentage change rather than raw dollar change, valuation ratios, volatility or beta, dividend yield, and sector. A $5 move in a $500 stock (1%) is smaller than a $5 move in a $50 stock (10%).

## Sectors
The market is commonly divided into 11 GICS sectors, including information technology, health care, financials, consumer discretionary, consumer staples, energy, industrials, materials, utilities, real estate and communication services. Sectors respond differently to economic cycles and interest rates.

## Fundamental vs technical analysis
Fundamental analysis evaluates a company's financial statements, competitive position and valuation to estimate intrinsic value. Technical analysis studies price and volume patterns, such as moving averages. A 50-day or 200-day moving average smooths price data to show trends.

## Market indexes
The S&P 500 tracks about 500 large U.S. companies weighted by market cap. The Dow Jones Industrial Average tracks 30 large companies weighted by price. The Nasdaq Composite is heavy in technology. Index levels give a quick read of broad market direction.

## Caution about short-term moves
Daily price changes are driven by news, sentiment and noise and are hard to predict. Single-day moves rarely say much about a company's long-term value.
